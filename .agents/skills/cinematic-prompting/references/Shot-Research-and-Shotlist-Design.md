# Shot Research and Shot-List Design

## Purpose

Use this reference when the user has a strategic guideline, theme, destination, product story, manifesto, or partial script but does not yet know what the piece should show.

Shot Research answers **what is relevant and visually valuable to show**. Shot architecture later decides **how those selected ideas become individual shots**. The Per-Shot Direction Check then decides **how each shot is framed or moved**.

Do not run this stage merely because more ideas are possible. Skip it when the supplied script or shot list already defines the visuals well enough, unless the user requests alternatives, enrichment, or factual verification.

## 1. Establish the research mandate

Extract or confirm:

- communication objective and audience;
- message, promise, or argument the sequence must support;
- geographic, cultural, historical, product, or category scope;
- launch date, time period, and factual claims that may change;
- desired emotional arc and approved visual direction;
- required subjects, moments, locations, products, or brand assets;
- topics, stereotypes, claims, locations, or representations to avoid;
- intended shot count, duration, format, and production/generation constraints;
- balance between literal proof, human observation, symbolism, and spectacle.
- real-world occupancy, public-life patterns, staff/service roles, and other background behavior needed to keep proposed locations plausibly lived-in.

When the mandate is broad, culturally sensitive, or capable of producing very different stories, present a compact research plan before deep research. If the mandate is already clear, state the plan and proceed without turning it into an unnecessary interview.

## 2. Research in layers

Search only the layers that can materially improve the piece:

1. **Core facts:** dates, route or product facts, geography, terminology, historical context, and current claims.
2. **Human relevance:** rituals, behaviors, aspirations, relationships, everyday details, and secondary people whose activity makes the world feel inhabited rather than staged for one hero.
3. **Place and culture:** architecture, cuisine, music, craft, landscape, climate, visual codes, public life, realistic population density, and documented connections between locations or communities.
4. **Brand relevance:** existing campaign language, product truths, service experience, audience expectations, and legal or identity constraints.
5. **Visual opportunity:** actions, textures, transitions, contrasts, parallels, transformations, and spatial relationships that can become images rather than exposition.
6. **Feasibility:** reference availability, generation difficulty, continuity burden, rights, cultural sensitivity, and whether live action, archive, graphics, or post-production would be more reliable.

Use current web research when facts, routes, products, places, events, or platform capabilities may have changed. Prefer primary and authoritative sources for factual claims: official brand materials, government or tourism bodies, cultural institutions, museums, archives, academic sources, and direct documentation. Use high-quality secondary sources to add context or discover leads, not as a substitute for verification.

If browsing or source access is unavailable, say what could not be verified. Mark unverified ideas as hypotheses or creative leads; never present them as facts.

## 3. Protect cultural and factual integrity

- Do not reduce a place or community to postcard landmarks, food clichés, costumes, or generic stock imagery.
- Distinguish documented cultural relationships from superficial visual resemblance.
- Look for specific human behavior and lived texture alongside recognizable icons.
- Do not research a busy public or social location and then depict it as conveniently empty. Note realistic patterns of occupancy and background activity by place, time, weather, and context.
- Check whether a proposed symbol has political, religious, historical, or identity implications.
- Do not imply a route, service, partnership, first, record, or historical connection without a source.
- Separate source-backed facts from creative interpretations and symbolic associations.
- Record pronunciation, spelling, attribution, permissions, and naming requirements when relevant.

## 4. Build the Visual Research Board

Turn findings into candidate visual motifs. Each candidate must include:

- **Motif or moment:** what could be shown;
- **Relevance:** how it supports the message rather than merely decorating it;
- **Evidence:** source-backed fact, observed pattern, brand truth, or clearly labeled creative inference;
- **Visual potential:** action, composition, texture, contrast, transition, or emotional behavior;
- **Narrative role:** setup, proof, connection, escalation, contrast, release, or resolution;
- **Brand and audience fit:** why it belongs in this specific piece;
- **Feasibility:** likely generation approach, reference needs, continuity burden, and production risk;
- **Occupancy plan:** expected background roles, activity, density, depth, and any narratively justified empty-space exception;
- **Sensitivity or cliché risk:** what could become inaccurate, reductive, or generic;
- **Source:** direct link or citation for factual material.

Reject candidates that are visually attractive but strategically irrelevant, redundant, unsupported, or too risky to represent responsibly.

## 5. Convert research into narrative routes

When several organizing ideas are plausible, propose two or three distinct narrative routes. A route is not a color palette or camera style; it is the logic that determines what the audience sees and in what progression.

Examples of narrative logic include:

- parallel daily rituals in two places;
- a journey from anticipation to arrival;
- contrasts that gradually reveal a shared value;
- one object, gesture, sound, or shape connecting environments;
- practical service proof expanding into emotional possibility;
- human micro-moments building toward a geographic reveal.

For each route state:

- central idea and one-sentence audience takeaway;
- opening, development, turn, and ending;
- strongest candidate motifs;
- how the approved mood/style supports it;
- originality and cliché risk;
- factual/cultural dependencies;
- generation and continuity implications;
- recommendation and rationale.

Obtain approval of one route, a combination, or a redirect before finalizing the proposed shot list.

## 6. Draft the proposed shot list

Build shots from the approved route and best research candidates. For each proposed shot provide:

- provisional scene and shot ID;
- concise visual description in plain language;
- narrative function and emotional beat;
- research basis or creative-inference label;
- continuity carried into or out of the shot;
- approximate duration or priority;
- likely static/video treatment;
- important reference or feasibility note;
- alternative when the concept is risky or difficult.

Sequence shots for meaning, escalation, contrast, breath, and editability. Do not create a checklist montage in which every research fact receives one disconnected image. Combine or discard motifs when they do not advance the piece.

The proposed shot list remains a creative recommendation until the user approves it. After approval, send it into production-bible and shot-architecture work. Camera position and movement are decided later in the Per-Shot Direction Check unless they are inseparable from the concept.

## 7. Deliverable format

Keep these sections distinct:

1. Research mandate and assumptions.
2. Sources consulted.
3. Verified findings.
4. Creative hypotheses or symbolic leads.
5. Visual Research Board.
6. Narrative route options and recommendation.
7. Approved narrative route.
8. Proposed shot list.
9. User decisions, rejected ideas, and unresolved risks.

Place citations beside the factual claims they support and preserve a source list in the report. Keep citations, research commentary, and uncertainty labels out of copy-ready generation prompts.

## Example research mandate: destination-route manifesto

For a hypothetical manifesto about a new direct route between Rio de Janeiro and Lisbon, do not jump immediately to beaches, landmarks, food, or tiled façades. First investigate:

- the verified route and launch facts;
- the campaign's actual strategic promise;
- documented historical and contemporary connections;
- meaningful similarities and differences in daily life;
- cuisine, architecture, landscape, language, music, and rituals that genuinely support that promise;
- less obvious human details that avoid a postcard montage;
- what GOL and the audience can credibly claim or feel;
- representation, rights, and cliché risks.

The result might support parallel rituals, a departure-to-arrival journey, or a human connection across two cities—but those routes should emerge from the research and brief, not from assumptions. Treat the premise and all current operational claims as facts to verify at project time.
